What is pre-IPO investing?

Pre-IPO investing means buying shares of a private company before it lists on a public stock exchange through an initial public offering (IPO). Instead of buying shares on the stock market, investors buy them privately, usually years before any public listing, if one ever happens.

How do individual investors get access to pre-IPO companies?

The most common routes are private placements (buying directly in a company's funding round), pooled vehicles such as special purpose vehicles (SPVs) and venture or growth funds that hold one or more private companies, and secondary transactions, where existing shareholders such as early employees or investors sell shares they already own.

Each route has different paperwork, fees, minimums, and transfer restrictions, so it is worth understanding exactly what you would own before committing money.

Do I need to be an accredited investor?

In most cases, yes. Most pre-IPO offerings are made under SEC rules that limit participation to accredited investors. Under the SEC's definition, an individual generally qualifies with income over $200,000 (or $300,000 together with a spouse or spousal equivalent) in each of the last two years with the expectation of the same this year, or a net worth over $1 million alone or with a spouse, not counting a primary residence. Holding certain securities licenses in good standing (Series 7, 65, or 82) also qualifies.

Some funds go further and require qualified purchasers, a higher standard that generally starts at $5 million in investments for an individual.

How much money does it take to invest in a pre-IPO company?

Minimums vary widely. Pooled vehicles may accept commitments in the tens of thousands of dollars, while direct investments and institutional funds can require far more. Whatever the minimum, the more important question is how much you can afford to have tied up for years, or lose entirely, without affecting your financial plan.

How long is my money tied up?

Often several years, and there is no set schedule. Your money typically stays invested until the company goes public, is acquired, or you find a buyer on a secondary market, which may require company approval and may only be possible at a discount. You should plan as though you will not be able to access the money for a long time.

What happens to my shares when the company goes public?

Your private shares generally become publicly tradable shares. However, existing shareholders are usually subject to a lock-up period after the IPO, commonly around 180 days, during which they cannot sell. The stock price can move significantly, up or down, during that time, so the value at the IPO is not necessarily what you will be able to sell for.

What if the company never goes public?

Many private companies never IPO. A company may be acquired, which can return more or less than you invested; it may stay private indefinitely; or it may fail, in which case you can lose your entire investment. An IPO is one possible outcome, not a promise.

What fees are involved in pre-IPO investing?

Fees depend on the structure. Funds and SPVs commonly charge a management fee and a share of profits known as carried interest, and some add upfront placement or administrative fees. Layered fees can meaningfully reduce your net return, so ask for a full breakdown of every fee before you invest.

How are pre-IPO shares valued?

Private companies do not have a daily market price. Values usually come from the most recent funding round, which often prices preferred stock with special rights, or from secondary market trades, which can be at a discount or a premium to that round. Common stock and preferred stock in the same company can be worth very different amounts, and a later funding round at a lower price (a down round) can reduce the value of earlier shares.

Can I invest in pre-IPO companies through an IRA?

It can be possible through a self-directed IRA with a custodian that holds private investments. These accounts have strict rules against prohibited transactions, and some investment structures can create taxable income inside the IRA. Because mistakes can be costly, review the arrangement with a tax professional before using retirement money.

How are pre-IPO investments taxed?

Gains are generally taxed as capital gains when you sell, with long-term rates applying if you held the investment for more than one year. Fund and SPV investors often receive a Schedule K-1 rather than a 1099, and K-1s can arrive late in tax season. Special rules, such as the qualified small business stock exclusion, apply only in specific situations and often do not apply to shares bought secondhand. A tax advisor can explain how these rules apply to you.

How much of my portfolio should go into pre-IPO investments?

There is no single right answer, but pre-IPO investments are generally treated as a small, higher-risk slice of an already diversified portfolio rather than a core holding. The right amount depends on your goals, time horizon, liquidity needs, and ability to absorb a total loss, which is why it helps to talk it through with a financial professional.
